Beyond Sequence Order:
Syntax-Informed Positional Embeddings for Transformers

Haris Riaz · Hyungji Kim · Mihai Surdeanu

Computational Language Understanding (CLU) Lab, University of Arizona

August 2026

</> Code 📖 Paper (arXiv soon) 📝 BibTeX

Positional embeddings tell a Transformer where its tokens are. They say nothing about how those tokens relate. In “Please move this large file to another folder,” the verb move is bound to file (what to move) and folder (where) by syntactic dependencies that positional machinery cannot see — folder is six words away from its verb, and every positional scheme in use today only measures that distance, not the oblique arc that connects them.

Our paper asks a narrow question with a surprisingly reusable answer: if you have a cheap syntactic signal, where exactly should it enter the Transformer? We inject a seven-value tag prior — derived from a single dependency parse — into models spanning all three dominant positional-encoding families (absolute, relative, rotary), for both encoders and decoders, and sweep the injection site. The best recipe turns out to depend on the architecture, and the strongest variant doesn’t add syntax to the attention score at all: it rescales the position term by how well the query aligns with the key’s syntactic tag.

The headline numbers, all relative to a matched baseline with no syntactic supervision: +10.3% SyntaxGym, −9.0% perplexity (a metric nearly every prior syntax-injection method makes worse), and +8.2% on GLUE — while conditioning on one parse at inference instead of marginalizing over ~300.

1. How position enters attention (and why Inkling matters)

Strip a Transformer-XL attention logit down and it splits into two bilinear pieces:

Aij = ACij + BDij

The AC term asks “how much does my content match yours?” — the ordinary query·key product. The BD term asks “how much do I care about your offset?”:

BDij = ⟨qi + v,  WR ri−j⟩

— the query taking an inner product with a learned embedding of the relative distance i−j. This is the “coefficients trick” lineage that runs from Shaw et al. (2018) and the Music Transformer through Transformer-XL and DeBERTa: relative position is not a fixed bias but a content-conditioned score — each query gets to weigh each offset differently.

For a few years RoPE largely displaced this family in frontier decoders. Then, concurrent with our work, Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling — a 975B-parameter (41B active) open-weights MoE with a 1M-token context — and its positional choice is the interesting part:

“We find that encoding position with a relative positional embedding performs better and extrapolates better to longer sequences than the more widely adopted Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE).”

And it’s not the static per-distance bias of T5. In Inkling’s implementation, each token’s hidden state is projected into a small vector whose inner product with a learned per-offset embedding is added to the attention logits — a content × position interaction of exactly the BD form above. A frontier lab independently concluded that the coefficients trick is worth a trillion-parameter bet, and much of the community met relative PE for the first time that day.

Our paper lives one step further down this road. If the position pathway is already a learned, content-conditioned channel — why should it only know about distance?

2. Seven tags of syntax

We derive our prior from hexatagging, a linearization that converts a dependency parse into a sequence of per-word tags. Each word gets two: a terminal tag τ ∈ {2 values} that is essentially a directional indicator relating the word to its syntactic governor, and a non-terminal tag ν ∈ {5 values} from the word’s position in the binarized head tree. Two lookup tables — Eτ (2×D) and Eν (5×D), about 7·D parameters total, well under 0.01% of the model — turn the tags into vectors. Hover the sentence to see the raw material:

Hover a word.
The running example from the paper (“she watches funny videos”). The terminal tag encodes each word’s relation to its governor; the non-terminal tag comes from the binary head tree. Only a word’s first subword carries its tags.

Two details matter. The tags attach only to each word’s first subword, so a word contributes one tag signal no matter how the tokenizer shreds it. And the tag tables are trained with an auxiliary tag-prediction loss alongside the LM objective — masked positions have their tag inputs withheld, so the model must infer syntax from context, not copy it.

3. Where should syntax enter?

Here is the design space in one picture. A Transformer layer’s attention score has a content term and a position term; the input embedding feeds both. The hexatag prior can enter at three places — click each pathway:

Click a pathway above to see its equation and how it performs on Transformer-XL.
The four injection sites we study for a relative-PE decoder. Every arrow ends somewhere concrete: the input pathway adds to the token embedding, the positional pathway multiplies the position term, and both the disentangled term and the Shaw-style attention bias add directly to the attention scores.

Sweeping this space produces a clean ordering — and it isn’t the same for encoders and decoders:

Encoders (RoBERTa, DeBERTa-v3, ModernBERT): just add the tag vectors to the input embedding. The prior composes with whatever positional scheme the encoder already uses — absolute, relative, or rotary — and this simple recipe beats fancier ones downstream.

Decoders with relative PE (Transformer-XL): the input pathway is the weakest option. Syntax helps progressively more as it entangles with position: input-side < disentangled additive < multiplicative coupling.

4. Gate, don’t add

The winning decoder variant deserves its own section. We first form a syntactic analogue of the BD term — the same effective query, dotted against a projection of the key’s tag instead of an offset embedding:

cij = ⟨qi + v,  WE(Eτj + Eνj)⟩ / √d

One query, two questions: “how well do I align with this offset?” (BD) and “how well do I align with this tag?” (c). Then, instead of adding c to the score, we let it rescale the position term:

Ãij = ACij + (1 + cij) · BDij

The additive term injects the same syntactic push everywhere. The multiplicative correction scales with the positional preference the model has already formed — large where the query attends to the offset, silent where it doesn’t. With WE initialized near zero, c ≈ 0 and the model starts as an exact vanilla Transformer-XL.

Empirically this ordering is robust: the multiplicative form reaches SyntaxGym 80.60 (vs. 73.09 baseline) while cutting perplexity 18.63 → 16.95; the disentangled additive form lands at 78.72; input-side injection at 76.97. Syntax helps most when it sharpens an existing positional preference rather than shouting over it.

5. Results

Three claims, each with a figure.

First, the prior transfers downstream. On the decoder, fine-tuning the SiPE-pretrained Transformer-XL on GLUE lifts the macro average from 68.17 to 73.78 — +8.2% relative, with every individual task improving. On encoders the gains are smaller but consistent across all three PE families: RoBERTa +0.75%, DeBERTa-v3 +0.59%, ModernBERT +1.0% relative on the macro average, at both base and large scales.

Second, simplest fusion wins. Across an architecture sweep on RoBERTa — concatenation, learned interpolation, residual-stream injection, full 40-label dependency-relation embeddings — plain addition of the two coarse tags (ADD T+NT) attains the best macro-average. Third, the language-modeling tax is negative. Most syntax-injection methods pay for their syntactic gains with worse perplexity (TreeReg: 22.30; TPT variants: 45–48, against our baseline’s 18.63). SiPE’s multiplicative variant improves perplexity to 16.95 while matching or beating the parser-free methods on syntactic generalization — see the Pareto figure at the top of this post.

8. Relative PE is back — good timing

We built SiPE’s decoder story on Transformer-XL rather than a RoPE-era LLM architecture, and for most of the project that looked like a retro choice. Inkling makes it look prescient: a frontier lab just shipped a 975B-parameter model that drops RoPE for the Shaw-lineage relative PE, for exactly the reasons that made it a good injection target — the position pathway is a learned, content-conditioned score you can shape.

The correspondence is precise enough to spell out:

  • Inkling ≈ our disentangled variant. Inkling adds ⟨projected hidden state, per-offset embedding⟩ to the logits; our disentangled variant adds ⟨query, projected tag⟩ alongside BD. Same additive slot — the difference is what conditions the bias: a latent projection there, an explicit, parser-supplied, supervised syntactic tag here.
  • Our strongest variant goes one step further. Nothing in the Inkling recipe gates: its relative bias is strictly additive. Our best decoder result comes from multiplying the position term by the tag alignment — and additive vs. multiplicative is worth about two SyntaxGym points in our ablations. If the coefficients trick is coming back at frontier scale, the gate seems worth trying too.

Either way, the larger point stands on its own: the positional pathway is not a solved, frozen part of the architecture. It is a learned channel, it responds to content, and — as our results show — it responds to structure.

9. Limitations and what’s next

The biggest open problem is fast autoregressive generation. SiPE conditions on hexatags, and generating a new token in principle requires re-tagging the sequence so far; the parser is fast, but tags of earlier tokens can be revised as the sentence grows, which invalidates cached keys and values. Efficient incremental decoding under a per-step syntactic prior is the direction we care most about next. Our experiments are also bounded by an academic compute budget — small models, English, WikiText/BLLIP-scale pretraining — and our reported perplexity is conditioned on the single parse (that is the trade the Pareto figure makes explicit).
